# Compare chunkings at a fixed token budget of whole chunks, scored against labelled spans

Most published chunk-size studies compare chunkings at a fixed top-k, so a 1024-token chunking at k = 5 hands the reader sixteen times the text of a 64-token chunking at the same k, and larger chunks win partly because they carry more text. A pilot on QASPER showed how strong that artifact is: at k = 5, 512-word chunks beat 64-word chunks by about 0.56 to 0.61 recall for every question type, and at a fixed 512-word budget the sign flipped. The same studies also count a chunk as relevant when it contains the answer string, or label relevance by chunk id, which only works for the one chunking the labels were made on.

So every chunking is compared at the same token budget, from 128 to 8192 tokens, counted with one tokenizer (tiktoken `cl100k_base`). The budget is filled with whole chunks in reranked order and never cut; a chunk bigger than the budget is not scored at that budget, and small-to-big returns a parent only when it fits the room left. Each budget is reported two ways: the production view caps the reader at the top 8 reranked chunks, as a production system would (with the top 5 as a side table), and the fair view drops the cap so every chunking gets as much text as fits. The production view is what decisions are judged on; the fair view shows how much of a gap comes from the cap alone, which binds only small chunks at large budgets. Relevance is labelled as spans of source text (document, character offsets, quote and source hash), so retrieval recall and precision can be computed for any chunking without relabelling. Fixed top-k results are produced only to reproduce the literature.
